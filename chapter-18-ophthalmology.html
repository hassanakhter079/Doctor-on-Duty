Hordeolum (Stye) & Chalazion (Meibomian Cyst)

Acute localized staphylococcal abscess of eyelid glands (Zeis/Moll/Meibomian) vs chronic non-infectious lipogranuloma of meibomian gland; clinical distinction, warm compresses, topical Tobradex/Maxidex, and surgical incision & curettage.

1. Etiology, Pathophysiology & Differential

Hordeolum and chalazion represent the two most common localized inflammatory swellings of the eyelids encountered in outpatient practice:

FeatureHordeolum (Stye / گوہانجنی)Chalazion (Meibomian Cyst)
Pathology Acute suppurative staphylococcal micro-abscess. Chronic sterile granulomatous lipogranuloma due to obstructed meibomian gland duct.
Subtypes External: Glands of Zeis (sebaceous) or Moll (ciliary sweat).
Internal: Meibomian glands (deeper, points internally).		 Hard, rubbery, indolent nodule embedded within the tarsal plate; often secondary to resolved internal hordeolum.
Clinical Signs Marked focal tenderness, erythema, edema, localized purulent pointing pustule. Painful blinking. Non-tender or minimally tender, well-circumscribed, painless firm subcutaneous lump. Moves with tarsus.
Etiology Staphylococcus aureus (90–95%), associated with blepharitis, poor hygiene, rub trauma. Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), rosacea, seborrheic dermatitis, chronic blepharitis.

2. Red Flags & When to Escalate

  • Preseptal Cellulitis: Diffuse eyelid swelling, erythema, tenderness extending beyond orbital rim (requires oral/IV antibiotics).
  • Orbital Cellulitis: Proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, pain with eye movements, reduced visual acuity, afferent pupillary defect (emergent CT orbit + IV antibiotics).
  • Recurrent Chalazion at Same Site: Mandatory biopsy in elderly patients to rule out Sebaceous Gland Carcinoma.

3. Stepwise Pharmacotherapy & Management

Step 1 Hydrotherapy & Warm Compresses:

Clean warm moist compresses for 10–15 minutes, 3 to 4 times daily. Melts inspissated lipid plugs and promotes spontaneous pustule drainage. Strict prohibition of manual squeezing or popping.

Step 2 Topical Ophthalmic Antibiotics / Combinations:

Apply topical antibiotic ointment inside lower conjunctival fornix and over lid margin BD to TDS x 7 days:

  • Tobramycin 0.3% + Dexamethasone 0.1% (Eye Drop/Oint Tobradex) 1–2 drops TDS or ointment at bedtime.
  • Chloramphenicol 0.5% (Eye Drop/Oint Chloromycetin) TDS x 7 days.
  • Polymyxin B + Bacitracin + Neomycin (Eye Oint Polyfax Ophthalmic) applied along lid margin at night.
Step 3 Systemic Antibiotics (Indications: Surrounding Cellulitis or Severe Internal Hordeolum):
  • Co-amoxiclav (Tab Augmentin 625mg) 1 tablet TDS PO x 5–7 days.
  • In penicillin allergy: Clarithromycin (Tab Klaricid 500mg) 1 tablet BD PO x 7 days.
  • For chronic recurrent MGD/rosacea chalazia: Doxycycline (Cap Vibramycin 100mg) 1 cap daily PO x 4–6 weeks.
Step 4 Minor Surgical Intervention (Incision & Curettage):

Persistent chalazion (> 4–6 weeks conservative failure): Local infiltration with 2% Lignocaine, apply chalazion clamp, evert lid, make vertical incision through tarsal conjunctiva (parallel to meibomian glands to avoid transecting adjacent ducts), curette granulomatous contents thoroughly, pressure patch x 2 hours.

Diagnostic / Clinical Imaging
External Hordeolum (Stye) Acute focal staphylococcal micro-abscess pointing along the anterior ciliary eyelid margin.

Blepharitis (Anterior & Posterior MGD)

Chronic inflammatory condition of the eyelid margins divided into anterior (staphylococcal/seborrheic) and posterior (meibomian gland dysfunction); eyelid hygiene regimen, baby shampoo scrubs, topical antibiotics, and low-dose systemic tetracyclines.

1. Clinical Classification & Presentation

Blepharitis is a chronic, relapsing bilateral inflammation of the lid margin that affects up to 40% of ophthalmology clinic visits:

SubtypeAnatomical Site & PathophysiologyCharacteristic Clinical Findings
Anterior Staphylococcal Base of eyelashes (cilia). S. aureus or S. epidermidis infection and hypersensitivity. Hard, brittle fibrinous crusts around lash base (collarettes), ulcerated lid margin, trichiasis, madarosis (lash loss), poliosis.
Anterior Seborrheic Anterior lid margin. Associated with seborrheic dermatitis of scalp, eyebrows, and nasolabial folds. Greasy, soft flakes and scales glued to cilia; smooth shiny lid margin without ulceration.
Posterior Blepharitis (MGD) Meibomian gland orifices at posterior lid margin. Altered lipid composition & ductal keratinization. Capped/pouting meibomian orifices, thickened turbid "toothpaste-like" secretions on expression, foamy/frothy tear film, tear breakup time (TBUT) < 10s. Associated with Acne Rosacea.

2. The 3-Step Bedside Hygiene Cornerstone

1 Thermal Liquefaction (Warm Compresses): Apply clean warm moist towel over closed eyelids for 5–10 minutes BD. Melts meibum lipid plug (melting point raised from 32°C to 40°C in MGD).
2 Mechanical Lid Scrubbing: Dilute mild baby shampoo (1:10 in warm water) or commercial lid wipes (Blephaclean). Gently scrub base of lashes using lint-free cotton tip to debride crusts.
3 Meibomian Expression (Lid Massage): Firm rolling massage of upper lid downwards and lower lid upwards toward the margin to express stagnant gland contents.

Acute Bacterial Conjunctivitis

Acute onset ocular redness with copious mucopurulent discharge and matting of eyelids; causative organisms (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae), hyperacute gonococcal warning (corneal perforation risk), and first-line topical fluoroquinolones.

1. Etiology & Clinical Hallmarks

Acute bacterial conjunctivitis is a self-limiting but highly communicable inflammation of the conjunctiva:

Pathogens Common Isolates: Staphylococcus aureus (adults), Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae (children, associated with otitis media), Moraxella catarrhalis.
Symptoms Presentation: Sudden onset foreign body sensation, grittiness, mild burning, marked conjunctival injection (beefy red peripheral hyperemia fading toward limbus).
Signs Hallmark Discharge: Thick yellow/green mucopurulent discharge; eyelids characteristically "glued together" upon waking in the morning.
Safety Signs Normal Cornea & Vision: Visual acuity intact, cornea clear with sharp light reflection, pupils equal and normally reactive to light.

2. Critical Alert: Hyperacute Gonococcal Conjunctivitis

SIGHT-THREATENING EMERGENCY:

Hyperacute onset (< 12–24 hrs), torrential copious creamy purulent discharge re-accumulating within seconds of wiping, marked chemosis, severe lid edema, and tender preauricular lymphadenopathy in a sexually active adolescent/adult indicates Neisseria gonorrhoeae. N. gonorrhoeae can rapidly penetrate intact corneal epithelium within 24–48 hours, causing corneal melting and perforation.

  • Immediate Treatment: Ceftriaxone (Rocephin) 1g to 2g IM/IV single dose + Azithromycin 1g PO single dose (covers co-infecting Chlamydia).
  • Saline ocular lavage hourly until discharge ceases + topical Ciprofloxacin/Moxifloxacin drops q1h. Urgent ophthalmology consult.

Viral Conjunctivitis (Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis)

Highly communicable adenoviral infection presenting with watery serous discharge, prominent palpebral conjunctival follicles, and tender preauricular lymphadenopathy; strict contact isolation, supportive cold compresses, and strict prohibition of unindicated topical steroids.

1. Clinical Features & Transmission

Viral conjunctivitis accounts for over 75% of all acute conjunctivitis cases. It is overwhelmingly caused by Adenovirus (serotypes 8, 19, 37 in Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis [EKC]; serotypes 3, 4, 7 in Pharyngoconjunctival Fever [PCF]):

Onset Bilateral Spread: Starts unilaterally and spreads to the fellow eye within 24 to 48 hours via direct autoinoculation.
Discharge Serous / Watery: Profuse watery epiphora without true purulent pus. Morning crusting is thin and watery.
Pathognomonic Preauricular Node: Tender, palpable preauricular lymphadenopathy in front of the tragus is strongly suggestive of viral etiology.
Lid Exam Follicular Reaction: Rice-grain-like lymphoid follicles on the inferior tarsal conjunctiva; chemosis, subconjunctival petechial hemorrhages.

2. Critical Diagnostic & Prescribing Pitfalls

STRICT WARNING AGAINST EMPIRIC TOPICAL STEROIDS:

Do NOT prescribe topical corticosteroids (e.g. Betamethasone, Dexamethasone) in primary care for acute red eye. If the underlying cause is Herpes Simplex Keratitis (HSV), topical steroids cause rapid amoeboid enlargement of dendritic ulcers, corneal melting, and catastrophic secondary glaucoma or blindness. Steroids in adenoviral EKC prolong viral shedding and induce rebound keratitis.

3. Evidence-Based Treatment & Infection Control

Step 1 Infection Control & Quarantine: Highly contagious via eye secretions, towels, pillows, and doorknobs for 10–14 days. Frequent handwashing with alcohol sanitizer; separate towels; stay home from work/school for 7–10 days while tearing persists.
Step 2 Symptomatic Cold Hydrotherapy: Chilled sterile saline compresses applied to closed lids for 10 minutes 4 times daily significantly reduces lid edema, burning, and chemosis.
Step 3 Ocular Lubricants & Decongestants:
  • Chilled Preservative-Free Artificial Tears (Tears Naturale II, Refresh Tears) 1 drop q2-3h. Flushes viral particles and soothes ocular surface.
  • Topical antihistamine/decongestant (Eye Drop Naphcon-A) 1 drop BD x 3–5 days only for intense itching/edema.
Step 4 Subepithelial Infiltrates (SEIs) Management: EKC may develop nummular subepithelial corneal infiltrates after 2–3 weeks, causing glare and photophobia. If vision is significantly reduced, an ophthalmologist may initiate weak topical steroid (Fluorometholone FML 0.1%) with slow taper.
Diagnostic / Clinical Imaging
Epidemic Keratoconjunctivitis (EKC) Watery serous epiphora, palpebral follicular hyperplasia, and chemosis characteristic of adenoviral infection.

Allergic Conjunctivitis & Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis

Type I IgE-mediated ocular hypersensitivity ranging from seasonal allergic conjunctivitis to severe vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC); hallmark itching, stringy mucoid ropiness, giant cobblestone papillae, Horner-Trantas dots, and dual-action mast cell stabilizers.

1. Clinical Spectrum & Pathophysiology

Ocular allergies represent IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation releasing histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins:

EntityPatient Demographic & SeasonClinical Hallmark Signs
Seasonal / Perennial Allergic Conjunctivitis (SAC / PAC) All ages, atopic history (allergic rhinitis, asthma, eczema). Seasonal pollens (spring/summer) or indoor allergens (dust mites, pet dander). Intense ocular itching (cardinal symptom), bilateral mild chemosis, milky translucent conjunctiva, watery to white stringy mucus, dark infraorbital skin (allergic shiners).
Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC / بہار کٹار) Young boys (3–12 years), hot dry climates (Mediterranean, Pakistan/South Asia), spring/summer exacerbations. Severe itching, intense photophobia, foreign body sensation, thick ropy elastic mucus, giant "cobblestone" papillae (> 1mm) on superior tarsus, gelatinous limbal nodules with white eosinophilic Horner-Trantas dots, and non-healing shield ulcers on superior cornea.

2. Stepwise Treatment Protocol (Pakistani Brands)

Step 1 Non-Pharmacological Measures:

Cold compresses (vasoconstricts, relieves itching), strict avoidance of rubbing (rubbing degranulates further mast cells and risks keratoconus), chilled preservative-free artificial tears (Refresh Plus, Systane) to dilute allergens.

Step 2 Dual-Action Antihistamine + Mast Cell Stabilizers (First-Line):
  • Olopatadine 0.1% / 0.2% (Eye Drop Pataday / Opat): 1 drop once or twice daily. Rapid symptom relief within 30 minutes plus durable mast cell stabilization.
  • Ketotifen 0.025% (Eye Drop Zaditen / Ketot): 1 drop BD.
  • Epinastine 0.05% (Eye Drop Relestat): 1 drop BD.
Step 3 Oral Antihistamines (Adjunctive):
  • Levocetirizine 5mg (Tab T-Day / Xyzal): 1 tablet OD at bedtime.
  • Fexofenadine 120mg / 180mg (Tab Telfast): 1 tablet OD in morning.
Step 4 Severe VKC Refractory Regimen (Specialist Management):
  • "Soft" Topical Steroid Pulse: Fluorometholone 0.1% (FML) or Loteprednol etabonate 0.5% (Lotemax) QDS x 1–2 weeks with intraocular pressure monitoring.
  • Topical Cyclosporine 0.05%–0.1% (Eye Drop Restasis / Ikervis) or Tacrolimus 0.03% ointment: Steroid-sparing immunomodulator for chronic severe VKC.
Diagnostic / Clinical Imaging
Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) Everted upper eyelid displaying giant cobblestone papillae and thick ropy mucoid discharge.

Chlamydial Conjunctivitis & Trachoma

Adult inclusion conjunctivitis (Chlamydia trachomatis serovars D–K) sexually transmitted ocular infection vs endemic trachoma (serovars A–C); prominent inferior fornix follicles, peripheral corneal pannus, mandatory single-dose oral Azithromycin, and the WHO SAFE strategy.

1. Pathogenesis & Clinical Spectrum

Chlamydia trachomatis causes two clinically distinct ocular conditions depending on the serovar group and transmission route:

ConditionSerovars & TransmissionPathognomonic Clinical Signs
Adult Inclusion Conjunctivitis (AIC) Serovars D through K. Oculogenital transmission via autoinoculation from genital secretions or contaminated swimming pools in sexually active young adults. Subacute to chronic unilateral or bilateral red eye refractory to standard topical antibiotics. Mucopurulent discharge, large follicles in inferior fornix, nontender preauricular lymph node, superficial punctate keratitis, micropannus. Urethritis or cervicitis frequently co-exists.
Trachoma (رکوما / آشوب چشم اندیمک) Serovars A, B, Ba, and C. Spread by flies (Musca sorbens), eye-seeking gnats, direct contact, and fomites in overcrowded, dry, impoverished environments. Leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide. WHO Grading: TF (Trachomatous Follicular: ≥ 5 follicles on upper tarsus), TI (Trachomatous Intense), TS (Trachomatous Scarring: Arlt's line on tarsal conjunctiva), TT (Trachomatous Trichiasis: lashes rubbing cornea), CO (Corneal Opacity). Limbal follicles leave sunken scars (Herbert's pits).

2. Definitive Antimicrobial Regimen

SYSTEMIC THERAPY IS MANDATORY:

Topical antibiotics alone are completely ineffective for adult inclusion conjunctivitis because the chlamydial reservoir resides in the genital tract and nasopharynx.

First-Line Azithromycin (Single High Dose):

Tab Azithromycin (Zithromax / Azomax 500mg): 1 g orally as a single dose (2 x 500mg tablets). 95% eradication rate; directly supervised therapy ensures compliance.

Alternative Doxycycline (Extended Regimen):

Cap Doxycycline (Vibramycin 100mg): 100 mg orally twice daily with meals x 14 days. Avoid in pregnancy, lactation, and children < 8 years.

Adjunctive Topical Ophthalmic Formulation:

Topical Erythromycin 0.5% or Tetracycline 1% eye ointment applied inside lower lid QDS x 3–4 weeks accelerates surface clearing.

Mandatory Sexual Partner Treatment:

All sexual partners within the preceding 60 days must be referred and treated empirically with Azithromycin 1g PO to prevent Ping-Pong reinfection.

3. WHO SAFE Strategy for Trachoma Control

S: Surgery for trichiasis (bilamellar tarsal rotation). A: Antibiotics (mass distribution of single-dose oral Azithromycin 20 mg/kg up to 1g). F: Facial cleanliness. E: Environmental improvement (sanitation, water access, fly control).

Diagnostic / Clinical Imaging
Trachomatous Follicular Inflammation Prominent lymphoid follicles on superior tarsal conjunctiva characteristic of WHO Trachoma Stage TF.

Corneal Abrasion & Traumatic Epithelial Defect

Mechanical disruption of corneal epithelium; extreme pain, photophobia, blepharospasm, diagnosis via slit-lamp fluorescein dye examination under cobalt blue filter, strict contraindication of home topical anesthetics, and prophylactic antibiotic coverage.

1. Clinical Presentation & Physical Examination

The cornea is the most densely innervated surface tissue in the human body (trigeminal nerve CN V1 ophthalmic division). An epithelial defect exposes naked nerve endings, precipitating intense distress:

History Traumatic Event: Fingernail scratch, paper cut, makeup brush, tree branch, contact lens overwear, or foreign body.
Symptoms Triad of Distress: Severe sharp ocular pain, intractable foreign body sensation with every blink, intense photophobia, lacrimation, and involuntary blepharospasm.
Diagnostic Fluorescein Staining: Instill 1 drop of Proparacaine/Lignocaine to break spasm, apply fluorescein dye strip moistened with saline. Under Cobalt Blue light, the epithelial defect fluoresces brilliant green where bare basement membrane is exposed.
Slit-Lamp Seidel Test (Globe Integrity): Verify absence of corneal stromal infiltrate (white ulcer), check anterior chamber depth, and confirm negative Seidel test (no aqueous streaming or leakage diluting fluorescein).

2. The Golden Rule of Emergency Ophthalmology

NEVER PRESCRIBE OR DISPENSE TOPICAL ANESTHETICS TO TAKE HOME:

Topical anesthetics (Proparacaine, Alcaine, Tetracaine, Lignocaine) eliminate corneal pain miraculously in the clinic. However, repeated self-administration is severely toxic to corneal keratocytes, inhibits epithelial mitosis, induces neurotrophic keratitis, leads to painless corneal melting, secondary bacterial ulceration, and permanent blindness.

3. Evidence-Based Treatment Protocol

1 Prophylactic Antibiotic Coverage:
  • Non-Contact Lens Wearers: Eye Drop Moxifloxacin 0.5% (Vigamox) or Tobramycin 0.3% (Tobrex) 1 drop QDS x 5 days + Polyfax Eye Ointment at bedtime.
  • Contact Lens Wearers (High Pseudomonas Risk): Eye Drop Ciprofloxacin 0.3% (Ciloxan) or Ofloxacin 0.3% (Flox) q2h while awake. Discontinue all lens wear until fully healed.
2 Ciliary Spasm Relief (Cycloplegia):

Instill 1 drop of Cyclopentolate 1% (Eye Drop Cyclogyl) in clinic. Paralyzes ciliary muscle and sphincter pupillae, eliminating deep aching throbbing ciliary spasm pain and photophobia.

3 Systemic Analgesia:

Oral NSAIDs: Tab Synflex 550mg (Naproxen) 1 tablet BD or Tab Brufen 400mg (Ibuprofen) TDS with meals x 48 hours.

4 Eye Patching Controversy:

Routine eye patching is NO LONGER RECOMMENDED. Clinical trials prove patching does not accelerate epithelial healing or reduce pain, but creates an anaerobic chamber increasing bacterial infection risk (strictly prohibited in contact lens wearers or vegetable matter trauma).

Ocular Chemical Burns (Acid vs Alkali Emergency)

True ophthalmic emergency requiring immediate copius lavage prior to history or examination; alkali (liquefactive necrosis, rapid stromal penetration) vs acid (coagulative necrosis); Morgan lens irrigation until pH neutralizes (7.0–7.4), Roper-Hall grading, and limbal ischemia management.

1. Pathophysiology: Alkali vs Acid Burns

Chemical injury represents 10–20% of ocular trauma. Immediate emergency management takes precedence over visual acuity testing, clinical history, or registration:

ParameterAlkali Burns (Severe / Sight-Threatening)Acid Burns (Self-Limiting Penetration)
Common Agents Lime/cement (CaOH), drain cleaners/lye (NaOH), ammonia (NH3, fertilizer/refrigeration), bleach (NaOCl). Battery acid (H2SO4), pool cleaner (HCl), nitric acid (HNO3), acetic acid.
Mechanism Liquefactive Necrosis: Saponifies cell membrane fatty acids, destroys cellular architecture, penetrates anterior chamber within minutes. Coagulative Necrosis: Denatures and precipitates tissue proteins, forming a protective barrier that impedes deeper penetration (exception: Hydrofluoric acid).
Limbal Ischemia Destroys limbal stem cells (palisades of Vogt) & vascular arcade → blanched "porcelain" white eye → total stem cell deficiency and corneal melting. Ground-glass corneal appearance; limbal vascular arcade usually preserved unless very concentrated.

2. Immediate Resuscitation: The 5-Step Emergency Protocol

Step 1 Immediate Topical Anesthesia:

Instill 1–2 drops of Proparacaine 0.5% or Lignocaine 2% into conjunctival sac immediately to overcome intense blepharospasm.

Step 2 Continuous High-Volume Irrigation:

Irrigate immediately with 2 to 3 Liters of Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) or Ringer's Lactate using an IV infusion set, wide tubing, or Morgan Lens. Run continuously for at least 30 to 45 minutes.

Step 3 Double Eversion & Fornix Debris Sweeping:

Evert upper and lower eyelids. Particulate alkali (lime, plaster, cement granules) trapped in superior fornix will continuously leach alkali. Sweep fornices thoroughly with a moistened cotton bud to mechanically dislodge all particles.

Step 4 pH Monitoring with Litmus Paper:

Check conjunctival sac pH using litmus/pH paper. Wait 5 minutes after stopping irrigation before checking. Irrigation continues until neutral pH (7.0 to 7.4) is achieved and stable.

Corneal & Subtarsal Foreign Bodies

Evaluation and safe removal of superficial metallic, organic, and mineral corneal foreign bodies; subtarsal lid eversion, bevel-up needle/burr extraction, rust ring removal, Seidel test, and secondary keratitis prevention.

1. Clinical Assessment & History

Corneal foreign bodies (CFB) represent one of the most frequent industrial and domestic eye presentations:

Mechanism High-Velocity Risk: Hammering chisel, metal grinding, drilling, weed trimmers. High-velocity fragments risk intraocular foreign body (IOFB) and full-thickness penetrating globe rupture.
Symptoms Sharp Foreign Body Sensation: Constant scraping, lacrimation, photophobia, circumcorneal ciliary injection.
Lid Exam Mandatory Lid Eversion: A foreign body lodged on the subtarsal conjunctiva of the upper eyelid causes characteristic linear vertical scratch abrasions on the superior cornea during blinking.
Seidel Test Rule Out Perforation: Full slit-lamp examination: pupil peaked toward wound, flat anterior chamber, or positive Seidel test indicates ruptured globe (requires rigid eye shield, NPO, and emergent vitreo-retinal surgery).

2. Stepwise Bedside Removal Technique

1 Anesthesia & Patient Positioning:

Instill 1–2 drops of Proparacaine 0.5% (Alcaine). Position patient comfortably with forehead firmly against slit-lamp rest. Instruct patient to fixate steadily on a distant target.

2 Sterile Needle Technique:

Use a sterile 25-gauge or 27-gauge needle mounted on a 1 mL or 3 mL syringe (for tactile grip). Approach the cornea obliquely (tangentially) with the needle bevel facing up. Carefully lift the foreign body off the surface. Never point needle tip perpendicularly toward the pupil.

3 Metallic Rust Ring Management:

Iron particles form a surrounding orange-brown rust ring within 2–4 hours. If superficial, remove with an ophthalmic rotating dental burr (Algerbrush). If deep in visual axis, leave for 24–48 hours to soften and auto-extrude toward surface for safe re-attempt.

4 Post-Extraction Care:

Treat resulting epithelial defect as a corneal abrasion: Eye Drop Vigamox (Moxifloxacin) QDS x 5 days + Polyfax Eye Ointment at night. Ensure Tetanus Toxoid (TT 0.5mL IM) booster if soiled/rusty object and > 5 years since last vaccination.

Diagnostic / Clinical Imaging
Corneal Metallic Foreign Body Superficial metallic iron splinter embedded in corneal stroma with early surrounding orange rust ring.
pp. 454–456 Sight-Threatening Emergency Glaucoma Emergencies Topic 110

Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (AACG Emergency)

Ophthalmic hyper-emergency caused by pupillary block and acute occlusion of the iridocorneal drainage angle; sudden severe ocular/hemicranial pain, colored haloes around lights, steamy cornea, fixed mid-dilated pupil, stony-hard globe (IOP > 40–70 mmHg), immediate medical quadri-therapy, and definitive Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI).

1. Pathophysiology, Triggers & Clinical Pentad

Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (AACG) is an acute ocular crisis where aqueous humor outflow through the trabecular meshwork is abruptly blocked by apposition of the peripheral iris against the trabeculum:

Predisposition Anatomical Risk Factors: Hyperopia (small crowded anterior chamber, short axial length), shallow anterior chamber, thick anteriorly displaced crystalline lens, elderly females, Asian/Inuit descent.
Triggers Mydriasis Precipitant: Dim lighting (cinemas, dusk), emotional stress, or mydriatic/anticholinergic medications (Atropine, Tropicamide, Amitriptyline, Ipratropium nebulizers, SSRIs, pseudoephedrine).
Symptoms The AACG Symptom Complex: Sudden excruciating unilateral peri-orbital/hemicranial pain, blurred visual drop, rainbow-colored haloes around lights, severe nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain (frequently misdiagnosed as acute abdomen or migraine).
Physical Signs Pathognomonic Pentad: (1) Injected conjunctiva (ciliary flush), (2) Microcystic corneal epithelial edema ("steamy ground-glass cornea"), (3) Shallow anterior chamber, (4) Vertically oval, mid-dilated (4–6mm), fixed non-reactive pupil, (5) Stony-hard globe on gentle digital ballottement through closed lids (IOP elevated to 40–70 mmHg; normal 10–21 mmHg).

2. Immediate 4-Step Medical Resuscitation Protocol

The immediate objective is rapid reduction of IOP to < 30 mmHg to reverse corneal ischemia and papillary paralysis before definitive laser intervention:

3. Definitive Bilateral Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI)

BILATERAL LASER IRIDOTOMY (CURATIVE):

Medical therapy is temporary. Once the cornea clears, Nd:YAG Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) creates a full-thickness micro-aperture in the superior peripheral iris. This equalizes pressure between posterior and anterior chambers, permanently resolving pupillary block.

Crucial Rule: The contralateral fellow eye has an identical anatomical narrow angle and faces a 50% risk of acute attack within 5 years. Prophylactic Nd:YAG iridotomy must be performed on the unaffected fellow eye during the same admission.

Anterior Uveitis (Acute Iritis)

Inflammation of the iris and anterior ciliary body; deep aching ocular pain, photophobia, circumcorneal ciliary injection, keratic precipitates (KPs), anterior chamber cell and flare, association with HLA-B27 and seronegative spondyloarthropathies, intensive topical Pred Forte, and therapeutic cycloplegia.

1. Clinical Presentation & Slit-Lamp Signs

Anterior uveitis (iritis / iridocyclitis) represents inflammation of the anterior vascular uveal tract (iris and pars plicata of ciliary body):

Symptoms Deep Pain & Photophobia: Dull, aching, throbbing deep ocular and brow ache. Intense consensual photophobia (shining light into the unaffected fellow eye induces pain in the diseased eye due to pupillary constriction).
Flush Ciliary / Circumcorneal Injection: Violaceous, dusky red flush surrounding the limbal corneal margin (in contrast to peripheral conjunctival injection).
Corneal Endothelium Keratic Precipitates (KPs): Clusters of inflammatory cells on the posterior corneal endothelium (fine/stellar in non-granulomatous; large greasy "mutton-fat" in granulomatous/sarcoidosis/TB).
Anterior Chamber Cell & Flare / Hypopyon: Leukocytes floating in aqueous beam (cells) and protein leakage through damaged blood-aqueous barrier (flare). Severe cases form a layered white exudate (hypopyon). Small, irregular, sluggish pupil due to iris adhesions (posterior synechiae).

3. Acute Pharmacotherapy (Pakistani Brands)

Cornerstone 1 Intensive Topical Corticosteroid:

Prednisolone acetate 1% (Eye Drop Pred Forte): 1 drop every 1 to 2 hours while awake initially. Highly lipophilic, penetrates corneal epithelium into anterior chamber to extinguish uveal inflammation. Taper gradually over 4–6 weeks as anterior chamber cells resolve.

Cornerstone 2 Therapeutic Cycloplegic / Mydriatic:
  • Atropine sulfate 1% (Eye Drop Atropine 1%) 1 drop BD, OR
  • Cyclopentolate 1% (Eye Drop Cyclogyl) 1 drop TDS.
  • Dual Action: Paralyzes ciliary muscle, relieving excruciating ciliary spasm pain; dilates pupil, preventing and breaking sight-destroying posterior synechiae (adhesions of iris to lens).
Cornerstone 3 Intraocular Pressure Monitoring:

Monitor IOP at every visit. Trabeculitis or steroid response can elevate IOP. Treat with topical beta-blockers (Betimol) or alpha-agonists (Alphagan). Avoid prostaglandin analogs (Latanoprost) which exacerbate uveitis.

Cornerstone 4 Systemic Steroids / Immunosuppressants:

Reserved for severe bilateral, posterior, or refractory intermediate/pan-uveitis: Tab Deltacortril (Prednisolone) 1 mg/kg/day PO tapered slowly, or Methotrexate/Adalimumab.

2. The 5 Cardinal Rules of Safe Eye Drop Administration

Rule 1 Wash Hands & Pull Lower Lid: Clean hands, tilt head back, pull lower eyelid down to form a pouch, instill exactly 1 drop. The conjunctival sac holds only 20–30 μL (a single drop); second drops simply overflow.
Rule 2 Nasolacrimal Occlusion (NLO): Close eyes gently and press index finger firmly against the inner corner of eye (lacrimal punctum) for 2 minutes. Reduces systemic vascular absorption by > 60% (crucial for Beta-blockers, Brimonidine, Atropine).
Rule 3 The 5-Minute Spacing Rule: If instilling more than one type of drop, wait at least 5 minutes between drops so the second does not wash out the first.
Rule 4 Drops Before Ointments: Always administer water-based eye drops first, wait 5 minutes, and apply oily eye ointments last.
